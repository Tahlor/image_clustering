Oral exam

Core machine learning

Calibration versus discrimination

Discrimination asks whether a score ranks correct or positive cases above others. Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies. A score can have strong AUROC and bad probability calibration, or vice versa. For routing, test the risk at the actual coverage threshold and by consequential subgroup.

ROC-AUC versus PR-AUC

ROC-AUC measures ranking across true- and false-positive rates and can look optimistic when negatives dominate. PR-AUC emphasizes precision and recall among the positive class and is often more informative for rare events. Neither selects the operating threshold; use workflow costs, capacity, and harm.

Leakage

Leakage occurs when a feature, split, or transformation exposes information unavailable at prediction time or allows related examples to cross validation boundaries. Prevent it with point-in-time features, grouped/temporal splits, fold-local preprocessing, lineage review, and skepticism toward surprisingly strong results.

Bagging versus boosting

Bagging trains models independently on resampled data and averages them to reduce variance; random forests add feature randomness. Boosting trains sequentially to correct prior residuals, often reducing bias but becoming more sensitive to noise and tuning. Choose from data size, nonlinearities, calibration, interpretability, latency, and operational constraints.

Distribution shift

  • Covariate shift: input distribution changes.
  • Prior shift: outcome prevalence changes.
  • Concept shift: relationship between input and outcome changes.

Monitor slices and outcomes, not only population-distance metrics; predefine recalibration, retraining, coverage restriction, or rollback.

Imbalance and asymmetric harm

Do not “solve” imbalance by accuracy. Establish the decision, error costs, review capacity, and label quality; use appropriate sampling or weighting; report PR curves and slice metrics; calibrate; and select thresholds from expected consequence.

Oral exam

Statistics and experimentation

Confidence interval

A frequentist 95% interval is produced by a procedure that would cover the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions. It does not mean a fixed parameter has a 95% probability of lying in this realized interval. Discuss sampling, dependence, multiple testing, and practical magnitude.

P-value

The probability, assuming the null and model assumptions, of observing a result at least as incompatible with the null as the one observed. It is not the probability the null is true and does not measure effect importance.

Power and minimum detectable effect

Power is the chance of rejecting the null for a specified true effect. MDE is the smallest effect a design can reliably detect at chosen alpha and power. Both depend on variance, sample size, allocation, clustering, compliance, and analysis plan.

Healthcare experiment design

  1. Define the workflow decision and estimand.
  2. Choose randomization unit to minimize contamination—case, reviewer, team, facility, or plan.
  3. Pre-specify primary outcome, safety/quality guardrails, duration, and MDE.
  4. Use intent-to-treat; separately report adoption and per-protocol descriptively.
  5. Account for clustering, interference, delayed outcomes, and repeated looks.
  6. Retain unbiased audits independent of model score.

When randomization is unavailable

Clarify the causal question; draw the data-generating process; identify confounders, mediators, colliders, and positivity; choose design before estimator—matching/weighting, interrupted time series, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, instrumental variables, or target-trial emulation; test assumptions and sensitivity.

RAG evaluation

Evaluate retrieval and generation separately: corpus coverage, policy/version correctness, recall@k, ranking, citation precision, groundedness, answer correctness, abstention, latency, cost, and workflow outcome. An answer can be fluent while retrieval is wrong.

Agents versus deterministic workflows

Use deterministic orchestration for known states, policy constraints, and consequential transitions. Add agentic choice only where flexible planning creates enough value, with constrained tools, schemas, budgets, audit trails, termination, and fallback. Do not call a prompt chain an agent merely for fashion.

LLM-as-judge

Useful for scale, but treat it as a model: validate against human adjudication, randomize order, test position/style/verbosity bias, use multiple rubric items, monitor drift, and never let the judge grade information it should not see.

Multimodal failure modes

  • Visual ambiguity and OCR-like homoglyph errors.
  • Language priors overriding pixels.
  • Layout/alignment failures and missing fields.
  • Resolution, crop, tile, and patch sensitivity.
  • Confident hallucination or semantic normalization when verbatim extraction is needed.
Oral exam

ML systems

Batch versus online

Batch maximizes throughput, cost control, auditability, and retryability; online supports interactive experiences but needs latency SLOs, capacity, fallback, and stale-data behavior. Many payer audits and queue-prioritization jobs are naturally batch; service-assistance workflows may require online deterministic controls.

Data contract and lineage

Define schema, grain, semantics, ownership, event and observation time, late corrections, null meaning, identifier stability, code/policy version, quality, access, backfill, and deprecation. Track source snapshots, transformation code, checks, and model inputs so incidents can be scoped and replayed.

Launch sequence

Retrospective benchmark → integration replay → shadow mode → mandatory-review pilot → canary/bounded expansion → broader deployment. A/B testing estimates causal effect; canary controls blast radius; shadow verifies integration without affecting decisions.

Observability

  • Data: volume, freshness, schema, nulls, source mix.
  • Model: audited quality, calibration, slices, OOD, abstention.
  • System: latency, availability, retries, cost, backlog, fallback.
  • Workflow: use, corrections, handle time, rework, appeals, burden, outcomes.

Delayed labels and feedback loops

Separate model decision, reviewer action, appeal, and eventual outcome; define maturity windows; backfill by original prediction date; retain random audit independent of score; log eligibility and alternatives so the model's own policy does not silently define future truth.

Senior SQL

Performance, physical design, and correctness

Define the grain → inspect the plan → identify dominant work → reduce rows, bytes, and movement → choose physical design for the workload → re-measure correctness and cost.

How to optimize a slow query

I first verify correctness and intended grain, because an accidental many-to-many join can be both slow and wrong. Then I identify the engine and workload and inspect the actual plan, row estimates, bytes scanned, spills, shuffles, lookups, and skew. I reduce work logically—filter, project, pre-aggregate—before choosing an index, partition, clustering or sort key, materialized intermediate, or schema change. Finally I benchmark representative parameters and reconcile row counts and financial totals, including write, storage, freshness, and maintenance costs.

Row store

Best for selective lookup or modification of a few complete rows, frequent transactions, and predictable low latency. B-tree indexes help when repeated selective predicates or joins avoid enough work to justify write and storage overhead.

Column store

Best for large analytical, append-heavy fact tables where queries scan many rows, select relatively few columns, aggregate, and benefit from compression, vectorized execution, and segment elimination.

When columnstore is a poor default

  • Singleton primary-key access dominates.
  • The table is small or short-lived.
  • Updates/deletes are heavy and hot.
  • Operational millisecond latency is central.
  • An analytical replica or warehouse would isolate workloads better.

What not to do

  1. Do not optimize before confirming the correct grain.
  2. Do not use DISTINCT to hide a many-to-many join.
  3. Do not index every filtered column.
  4. Do not assume a scan is a failure.
  5. Do not assume a CTE materializes.
  6. Do not wrap pruning/index columns in functions without checking the plan.
  7. Do not expect LIMIT to reduce columnar scan cost.
  8. Do not create thousands of tiny partitions.
  9. Do not force a hint from one parameter set.
  10. Do not use dirty reads as a generic speed fix for consequential data.
  11. Do not forget statistics, compaction, reclustering, vacuum/reorganization, refresh, and index retirement.

Cambia-style design

Use a partitioned/columnar claim-line history for daily analytical candidate generation, point-in-time enrollment/contract/policy joins, and reproducible feature snapshots. Use a row-oriented or filtered index for the small live open-review queue. Reconcile claim-line counts and paid dollars at every grain transition; a fast query with duplicate claim lines is a business failure.

Domain primer

Healthcare payer workflows

Core objects

  • Member/enrollment: who is covered, by which product, during what effective interval.
  • Benefit: covered services, cost share, exclusions, limits, and rules.
  • Provider/network/contract: identity, participation, rates, terms, and effective versions.
  • Claim and claim line: submitted codes, dates, units, charges, adjudication, payment, and adjustments.
  • Authorization: request, policy criteria, evidence, decision, and status.
  • Policy: versioned rules that may vary by plan/product/jurisdiction/effective date.

Payment integrity

Ensuring payment is accurate under coding, billing, contract, benefit, and policy rules. It includes prepay/postpay review, duplicate or incompatible billing, coding validation, contract accuracy, clinical-policy evidence, recovery, and provider/member burden. It is broader than fraud detection and should not be framed as “deny more claims.”

Prior authorization

A workflow for determining whether planned services satisfy coverage and medical-policy requirements. AI opportunities include document classification, evidence extraction, policy retrieval, completeness checks, routing, and summaries. Clinical or coverage authority remains with qualified accountable people and deterministic plan controls.

Why labels are dangerous

Historical decisions reflect workflow, access, documentation, reviewer behavior, coding, appeal, and policy—not pure ground truth. Missingness may indicate barriers or operational patterns. Reconstruct the data-generating process and preserve random adjudication before training or automating.

Research · multimodal GenAI · calibration

MLLM test-time augmentation

Black-box instability → controlled variants → sequence alignment → select for consensus → 71.2% to 75.2% → weaker grid warp adds diversity.

Recall bullets

  • 622 Pennsylvania death records; 3,684 name fields.
  • Padding, blur, noise, grid warp, resize, and temperature variants.
  • Progressive Needleman–Wunsch character alignment and voting.
  • Selected transforms by validation consensus, not isolated accuracy.
  • Field accuracy 71.2% → 75.2%; CER 9.0% → 7.2%.
  • Grid warp was individually worse but failed less correlatively.
  • 870 fields unanimous across 100 configurations; seven still wrong.

Example answer

I was trying to improve historical-record extraction from a closed multimodal model without fine-tuning or dependable internal confidence. The surprising issue was that tiny presentation changes produced different transcriptions. Rather than treat that only as instability, I generated controlled image variants and built a sequence-alignment consensus system. Initially it was tempting to choose the individually most accurate variants, but error analysis showed that a relatively weak grid-warp transformation improved the ensemble because it failed differently. I changed the selection objective to validation consensus performance and measured both accuracy and calibration. Exact field accuracy improved from 71.2% to 75.2%, with character error falling from 9.0% to 7.2%. The lesson was that in a compound system, marginal information and failure diversity can matter more than isolated model rank—and agreement still needs auditing because unanimous errors remain.
Scale · technical leadership · production

Seven million death records

A strong research model met production scale; the hard problem became throughput, retries, validation, confidence, review, cost, and deadline together.

Example answer

I served as technical lead for the research and engineering phase of a collection involving roughly seven million death records. At proof-of-concept scale, model quality was the visible problem; at production scale, every small inefficiency became expensive and every silent failure became material. I helped select and improve the MLLM approach, brought in TTA and confidence methods, evaluated batch interfaces, and treated retries, throughput, validation, review allocation, and cost as one system. Consequential automation · statistics · leakage

Belgium bounded-risk auto-approval

High average accuracy was the wrong question; the real question was how much review could be skipped at a defensible false-skip risk.

Example answer

I was asked to investigate whether RecordCount and DocType audit work could be reduced. The initial temptation was to optimize average classification accuracy, but that did not match the operational decision: a false skip is much more serious than sending another case to review. I reframed the project around coverage at bounded risk, used grouped splits, and aggressively blocked audit-value leakage. I compared prediction-shape, visual, text, embedding, classical ML, and foundation-model signals, then evaluated conservative operating points. On the evaluation data, roughly 78% of RecordCount images appeared skippable below about 2% false-skip risk, with a more conservative operating point around 62.5% at zero observed false skips; DocType showed roughly 66% coverage near 2% strict risk. The lesson was that consequential automation needs a policy curve and uncertainty, not one headline accuracy number.

VERIFY: sample size, interval interpretation, deployment status, and review-hours value.

Platform · evaluation · end-to-end ownership

Vantage

Example answer

Across document-AI projects, I noticed that the visible model differed but the hardest supporting work repeated: assembling predictions and audits, finding the matching images, computing metrics consistently, calibrating confidence, and choosing what humans should review. I designed Vantage as the reusable data-science layer for that work. Influence · architecture · economics

Transition to MLLM extraction

A promising new model was not enough; make the total-system case and close confidence, batching, cost, and deployment gaps.

Example answer

I helped lead a transition from a conventional collection-specific transcription stack toward multimodal foundation-model extraction. I did not argue that foundation models were better because they were fashionable. I benchmarked them on relevant records and compared accuracy, model-development time, deployment complexity, cost, and operational gaps. The results showed superior quality and potentially much faster iteration, but confidence and large-scale inference were legitimate blockers. I therefore built supporting work around test-time ensembling, confidence routing, and batch interfaces, and communicated the decision as a total-system business case. The broader lesson was that a new model earns architectural adoption only when the surrounding system—evaluation, cost, reliability, review, and delivery—also improves.

Hard technical problem · ambiguous labels

TRACE

Static image → several valid stroke trajectories → DTW alignment → adaptive targets → arbitrary-width lines → downstream synthesis.

Example answer

One of the hardest problems I worked on was recovering pen trajectories from static handwriting. The pixels reveal where ink exists but not necessarily the order or direction in which it was written, so the recorded trajectory can be only one of several valid answers. A point-by-point loss therefore punished visually faithful reconstructions when sampling or stroke order differed. I redesigned the system around a variable-width convolutional recurrent network and differentiable Dynamic Time Warping. I also added an adaptive-target stage that could reverse a stroke or swap adjacent strokes when that produced a more faithful reconstruction. The full model achieved an LPIPS reconstruction score of 0.106, compared with 0.296 for the same architecture without the DTW objective, and the recovered strokes could drive handwriting synthesis. The lesson was to verify that the label and loss represent the invariant outcome rather than one arbitrary annotation.
Data-centric ML · limited labels · building from scratch

DELINE8K

Pixel labels were expensive; existing synthetic data failed; build a modular distribution-engineering factory and prove ingredients through ablation.

Example answer

We needed semantic segmentation for historical forms, but real pixel labels were expensive and available synthetic datasets were too clean and usually distinguished only handwriting from printed text. I treated the dataset as the product. I defined a separate form-element class, assembled handwriting from multiple sources, collected more than ten thousand fonts and government forms, generated diverse historical backgrounds, and built a compositing and degradation pipeline for 8,000 labeled images. Early generated samples exposed absurd artifacts—coffee mugs, mountain drawings, and handwriting that lacked realistic diversity—so I inspected failures and changed the sampling distribution rather than only tuning the network. On the target benchmark, F-measure reached 0.876 for handwriting, 0.917 for printed text, and 0.791 for form elements; removing generated backgrounds reduced all three substantially. The lesson was that synthetic data succeeds as deliberate distribution engineering, not as a volume generator.
Reframing · unsupervised learning · reuse

Fine-grained form classification

The model was paying attention to handwritten answers; the class depended on the blank form. Remove the nuisance variable before learning representations.

Example answer

I was trying to cluster forms that had almost identical language and differed only in subtle structural details such as header height, column widths, or numbering. Generic embeddings kept seeing the handwritten answers and semantics instead of the reusable layout. I reused our DELINE8K segmentation model to preserve printed text and form elements while masking handwriting and background, then compared CLIP, DiT, ResNets, and target-trained masked autoencoders. I also raised the MAE resolution to 448 pixels and used translation augmentation because the relevant distinctions disappeared at common resolution while page placement should not define class. On the U.S. Census benchmark, K-means ARI improved from 0.343 to 0.610 with segmentation and to 0.768 with segmentation plus translation; KNN reached 0.980. The lesson was to remove a reliable nuisance variable explicitly instead of asking a larger model to discover the invariance indirectly.

Content Understanding and Data Explorer

Growth · prioritization · managing up

Ship the useful core sooner

General-solution instinct and high standards → slower adoption → explicit MVP, earlier partners, regular demonstrations, quantified progress, and stronger priority boundaries.

Example answer

A real weakness I have been working on is that my instinct is to solve the general technical problem, and that can make the path from a strong evaluation result to an adopted product longer than it needs to be. I saw that pattern in projects such as our evaluation platform and confidence-routing work: the technical foundation was strong, but I had not always defined the smallest end-to-end user path early enough. I now write explicit MVP scope and non-goals, involve engineering partners earlier around interfaces and deployment, demonstrate partial workflows regularly, and communicate provider or infrastructure risks with fallback options before they affect the schedule. I have also become more willing to decline side work that threatens the critical path. I would not claim this is completely solved; the change is that I now treat adoption and integration as part of the architecture rather than work that begins after research.
